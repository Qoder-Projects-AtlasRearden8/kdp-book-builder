# Ask Better Questions (Without Memorizing Prompts)

People collect prompts the way they collect recipes they never cook. A folder full of clever wordings feels productive, but it does not teach you what to do when the tool gives you a strange answer. A simple request structure is more useful than a hundred copied prompts.

You can remember this structure in four moves. Think of it as telling a capable new helper what you need, without assuming they already know your life.

## The four-part request

**1. Name the job.** Start with the result you want, not the topic you want to discuss. "Help me turn these notes into a three-item checklist" is a job. "Tell me about checklists" is a topic.

**2. Add the context that matters.** Give the tool the details a thoughtful colleague would need: who will read it, how long it should be, what happened already, and what must not change. You are not being fussy. You are preventing a confident guess from filling in the blanks.

**3. Describe the shape.** Say whether you want a paragraph, a table, a list, an email, or three options. If you have a tone in mind, name it plainly: "warm but professional," "short enough for a phone screen," or "explain it to someone who has never used this tool."

**4. Set the guardrails.** Tell the tool what to leave alone and what it should do when unsure. A few useful instructions go a long way:

- Do not invent facts, names, dates, or quotes.
- Mark anything uncertain instead of smoothing over it.
- Ask me for missing details before writing the final version.
- Keep the language plain and skip the hype.

You can combine these moves in plain language. It does not need to sound like a technical command.

```text
Job: Draft a friendly reminder email to parents about next week's
library book return.

Context: It's a small school library. The email goes out on Monday for
a Friday deadline. Some families speak English as a second language.
We don't know how many books are missing.

Shape: Under 150 words, short paragraphs, one clear action, no shame.

Guardrails: Don't invent a title, author, fee, or policy. If a detail is
needed, leave a clear [placeholder] instead of guessing.
```

That request does not require a special trick. It simply supplies the missing pieces before they can become wrong assumptions.

## What good looks like

A useful answer should fit the job, reflect the context, and follow the guardrails. Read it once for the big picture, then once for specifics. Check names, numbers, links, promises, and anything that would be embarrassing to send by mistake.

If the draft is close but not right, do not start over. Say what to keep and what to change:

> Keep the warm opening and the single action. Make the middle two sentences shorter. Remove the phrase "as you know," and do not mention a fee.

This kind of feedback is easier for both of you than "make it better." It points at a target.

## When the answer misses

Sometimes the tool produces something polished and still wrong. That does not mean you failed at prompting. It means the tool is doing what pattern machines do: filling gaps with plausible language. Your job is to notice the gap.

Try these repairs in order:

1. Re-state the job in one sentence.
2. Add the missing context, especially names, audience, and limits.
3. Ask for a smaller piece, such as an outline before a full draft.
4. Ask the tool to list its assumptions so you can correct them.
5. Stop using it for that part and do the thinking yourself.

That last option is not defeat. A helper is supposed to lighten the work, not take over your judgment.

## Keep a small notebook

When a request works well, save the request rather than only the answer. Add a note about why it worked: clearer audience, fewer invented details, a better length. Over a few weeks, you will build a personal pattern library that fits your actual life.

You do not need to memorize prompts. You need a repeatable way to say what you want, what you know, and where the tool should be careful. That habit travels with you from one AI tool to the next.

> **Try this today.** Take a message you need to write this week. Give it the four-part treatment: job, context, shape, and guardrails. Then edit the result as if a colleague drafted it. You are still the author. The tool only carried the first stack of papers.

This is the quiet goal of the whole field guide: use the tool without giving away your judgment, your privacy, or your voice. Start small, keep the human decisions human, and let competence arrive one useful draft at a time.
